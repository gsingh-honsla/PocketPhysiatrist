# Orthostatic Hypotension in Spinal Cord Injury

## Metadata
- **Category**: Spinal Cord Injury Medicine
- **Keywords**: orthostatic hypotension, spinal cord injury, autonomic dysfunction, neurogenic hypotension

## Core Pathophysiology and Clinical Presentation

Orthostatic hypotension (OH) is a common and potentially debilitating form of cardiovascular dysfunction following spinal cord injury (SCI), defined as a drop in systolic blood pressure of at least 20 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of at least 10 mmHg within three minutes of moving to an upright position. This condition is most prevalent and severe in individuals with complete lesions above the T6 neurological level, particularly during the acute phase of injury, with prevalence rates as high as 82% for tetraplegia versus 50% for paraplegia immediately post-SCI.

The primary pathophysiological mechanism involves disruption of sympathetic nervous system pathways responsible for cardiovascular regulation. In uninjured individuals, assuming an upright position causes blood to pool in the legs and abdomen due to gravity. Baroreceptors detect the resulting drop in blood pressure and trigger a reflex sympathetic response, causing vasoconstriction to increase peripheral resistance and maintain blood pressure. In SCI above T6, these descending sympathetic signals are blocked, preventing the normal compensatory vasoconstriction response. Consequently, blood vessels in the abdomen and lower extremities remain dilated, leading to significant venous pooling and a sharp fall in blood pressure upon sitting or standing. This pathophysiology is compounded by the loss of the skeletal muscle pump in paralyzed limbs, cardiovascular deconditioning from prolonged bed rest, and potential alterations in salt and water balance.

Clinical symptoms result primarily from cerebral hypoperfusion (insufficient blood flow to the brain) and can significantly impact rehabilitation participation and functional independence. The condition may persist chronically, with some individuals experiencing symptoms years after injury, particularly during morning mobilization or after prolonged periods of recumbency.

## Key Signs and Symptoms

- Dizziness or light-headedness upon position changes
- Syncope (fainting) or near-syncope episodes
- Visual disturbances (blurred vision, tunnel vision, or seeing spots)
- Generalized weakness or fatigue
- Nausea and pallor
- "Coat hanger" headache (pain in the neck and shoulders)
- Cognitive impairment or confusion during hypotensive episodes
- Reduced exercise tolerance and therapy participation

## Key Diagnostic Criteria and Classification Systems

### Diagnostic Criteria
- Systolic blood pressure drop ≥20 mmHg OR diastolic blood pressure drop ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing or head-up tilt to 60 degrees
- Symptoms may or may not be present (asymptomatic OH is common)
- Associated with neurological level of injury T6 or above
- Exclusion of other causes of hypotension (hypovolemia, medication effects, cardiac dysfunction)

### Classification System
| Type | Timing | Clinical Features | Management Implications |
|------|--------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Classic OH | Within 3 minutes of standing | Standard BP drop criteria met within 3 minutes | Most common type; responds well to standard interventions |
| Delayed OH | After 3 minutes of standing | BP drop occurs >3 minutes after position change | May indicate milder autonomic dysfunction; often improves with conditioning |
| Initial OH | Within 15 seconds | Transient BP drop (≥40 mmHg systolic or ≥20 mmHg diastolic) | Usually self-resolving; focus on gradual position changes |

*Note: This classification is adapted from general OH literature for conceptualization in SCI patients; not independently validated in SCI populations.*

## Comprehensive Rehabilitation Management

### Non-Pharmacologic Management
- **Immediate interventions**
  - Gradual mobilization protocol: begin with elevating head of bed, progress to reclining wheelchair, then tilt-table training
  - Abdominal binder application (highly effective for reducing splanchnic venous pooling)
  - Compression stockings (thigh-high or waist-high) to reduce lower extremity pooling
  - Patient education on avoiding rapid positional changes, especially morning mobilization
- **Long-term strategies**
  - Adequate hydration maintenance with liberalized salt intake (balanced with bladder/bowel management needs)
  - Physical counter-maneuvers: leg crossing, muscle tensing when symptomatic
  - Functional electrical stimulation (FES) to leg and abdominal muscles to activate muscle pump
  - Scheduling therapy sessions during afternoon hours when blood pressure may be naturally higher
  - Sleep positioning with head of bed elevated 15-30 degrees

### Pharmacologic Management
- **First-line medications**
  - **Midodrine**: 2.5-10 mg three times daily (maximum 30 mg/day); α-1 adrenergic agonist causing peripheral vasoconstriction; avoid doses after 5 PM to prevent supine hypertension; contraindicated in supine hypertension, severe cardiac disease
  - **Fludrocortisone**: 0.1-0.4 mg daily; mineralocorticoid that increases sodium and water retention; monitor for heart failure, hypokalemia, and peripheral edema; limited evidence for effectiveness in SCI population
- **Second-line medications**
  - **Droxidopa**: 100-600 mg three times daily (maximum 1800 mg/day); synthetic norepinephrine precursor; FDA-approved for neurogenic OH; give last dose ≥3 hours before bedtime; monitor supine blood pressure
  - **Atomoxetine**: 10-18 mg twice daily; norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor; limited data but may be more effective than midodrine in some studies; not FDA-approved for OH

### Interventional Procedures
- **Tilt-table testing**: Controlled assessment tool for diagnosis and monitoring treatment response; standardized protocol with continuous blood pressure monitoring
- **Transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation**: Emerging therapy showing promise for cardiovascular autonomic regulation; requires further research for clinical implementation
- **Epidural spinal cord stimulation**: Investigational approach for autonomic dysfunction in SCI; limited clinical data available

## Common Complications and Their Management

### Acute Complications
- **Syncope with falls**: Primary safety concern requiring immediate position change to supine; implement fall prevention strategies and consider wheelchair tilt-in-space features
- **Therapy session interruption**: May affect up to 43% of rehabilitation sessions; requires flexible scheduling and symptom monitoring protocols

### Chronic Complications
- **Supine hypertension**: Affects ~50% of patients on anti-hypotensive medications; monitor blood pressure in supine position; avoid late-day dosing; consider 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring
- **Cognitive impairment**: Associated with OH symptom burden in patients with injuries T6 and above; may improve with effective blood pressure management
- **Reduced quality of life**: Chronic symptoms can limit independence and participation; requires comprehensive approach including psychological support

## Related Assessment Tools

- American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale
- Functional Independence Measure (FIM)
- Orthostatic Hypotension Symptom Assessment (OHSA)
- Patient-reported orthostatic score for monitoring treatment response
- 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring for supine hypertension assessment
