# Autonomic Dysreflexia in Spinal Cord Injury

## Metadata
- **Category**: Spinal Cord Injury Medicine
- **Keywords**: autonomic dysreflexia, spinal cord injury, hypertensive crisis, sympathetic hyperreflexia, neurogenic bladder

## Core Pathophysiology and Clinical Presentation

Autonomic dysreflexia (AD) is a potentially life-threatening syndrome characterized by an uncoordinated and exaggerated sympathetic nervous system response to a noxious stimulus below the level of a spinal cord injury (SCI). This medical emergency occurs in individuals with SCI at or above the sixth thoracic vertebra (T6), as this level is above the major splanchnic sympathetic outflow. The higher the level of the spinal cord injury, the greater the risk, with up to 90% of patients with cervical or high-thoracic spinal cord injury being susceptible.

The pathophysiology involves a disruption of normal autonomic regulation. A noxious stimulus below the injury level (commonly a distended bladder) sends afferent signals to the spinal cord, triggering a massive, uncontrolled sympathetic reflex discharge. This causes widespread vasoconstriction below the level of injury, particularly in the splanchnic vascular bed, leading to a rapid and severe rise in blood pressure. T6 is of particular importance in the pathogenesis of autonomic dysreflexia. The splanchnic vascular bed is one of the body's largest reserves of circulatory volume and is controlled primarily by the greater splanchnic nerve. This important nerve derives its innervation from T5-T9. In an intact nervous system, baroreceptors would signal the brain to send descending inhibitory signals to counteract this hypertension. However, in an SCI at or above T6, these inhibitory signals are blocked at the site of injury. The brain can only mount a compensatory parasympathetic response above the level of injury, resulting in bradycardia, vasodilation, flushing, and sweating of the face, neck, and shoulders, while dangerous vasoconstriction persists below the injury.

After spinal cord injury, there is aberrant sprouting of primary afferents and propriospinal interneurons because of the upregulation of endogenous nerve growth factor and intraspinal soluble tumor necrosis factor-alpha, a proinflammatory cytokine. This maladaptive plasticity increases the excitability of the sympathetic spinal reflex, resulting in more severe bouts of autonomic dysreflexia once triggered.

The clinical presentation is defined by a sudden, significant increase in blood pressure—typically a rise in systolic pressure of 20-40 mmHg above the individual's baseline. Because the usual resting arterial BP in individuals with cervical and high thoracic SCI is approximately 15 to 20 mmHg lower than in able-bodied individuals, acute elevation of blood pressure to normal or slightly elevated ranges could indicate AD in this population. Some individuals may experience "silent" AD, with dangerous elevations in blood pressure but minimal or no symptoms.

## Key Signs and Symptoms

- **Pounding, severe headache** (most common initial symptom)
- **Profuse sweating and skin flushing** above the level of injury
- **Bradycardia** (slow heart rate), though tachycardia can also occur
- **Piloerection** (goose bumps) above the level of injury
- **Nasal congestion**
- **Blurred vision** or spots in the visual fields
- **Anxiety** or a feeling of impending doom
- **Cool, pale, dry skin** below the level of injury
- **Nausea and vomiting**
- **Increased spasticity**

## Key Diagnostic Criteria and Classification Systems

### Diagnostic Criteria
- Systolic blood pressure elevation ≥20-30 mmHg above baseline in adults
- For specific populations: ≥150 mmHg in adults, ≥140 mmHg in adolescents, ≥130 mmHg in children 6-12 years old, or ≥120 mmHg in children under 5 years old
- SCI at or above T6 level (though cases reported down to T10)
- Presence of triggering stimulus below the level of injury
- Characteristic autonomic signs and symptoms

### Classification System (For Conceptualization, Not Independently Validated)
| Severity | Blood Pressure Elevation | Clinical Features | Management Priority |
|----------|-------------------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Mild | 20-30 mmHg above baseline | Minimal symptoms, mild headache | Non-pharmacologic intervention |
| Moderate | 30-50 mmHg above baseline | Moderate headache, sweating, anxiety | Non-pharmacologic + monitoring |
| Severe | >50 mmHg above baseline or >150 mmHg systolic | Severe headache, risk of complications | Emergency pharmacologic intervention |

## Comprehensive Rehabilitation Management

### Non-Pharmacologic Management

#### Immediate Interventions
- **Sit the patient upright immediately**: Raise head of bed to 90 degrees or sit with legs dangling to utilize orthostatic effects
- **Loosen restrictive items**: Remove tight clothing, abdominal binders, or compression stockings
- **Monitor blood pressure**: Check every 2-5 minutes until episode resolves
- **Never lay the patient flat** (contraindicated during acute episode)

#### Identify and Remove Noxious Stimulus
- **Check bladder first** (85% of cases): Examine catheter for kinks or blockages; irrigate with small amount of sterile saline or change immediately; if no catheter present, insert using lidocaine jelly
- **Check bowel second**: Digital rectal exam for fecal impaction using topical anesthetic to prevent worsening hypertension
- **Perform full body skin examination**: Inspect for pressure injuries, ingrown nails, burns, or other triggers

### Pharmacologic Management

#### First-line Medications
- **Immediate-release nifedipine**: 10 mg capsule, may repeat once in 20-30 minutes
- **Nitroglycerin paste**: Apply 1-2 inches above level of injury every 2 hours; can wipe off if blood pressure stabilizes
- **Selection rationale**: Rapid onset and short duration crucial as blood pressure can drop quickly once stimulus removed

#### Second-line Medications
- **Hydralazine**: 10-20 mg IV for severe, refractory cases
- **Captopril**: 25 mg sublingual for sustained hypertension
- **Phenoxybenzamine**: For recurrent episodes (prophylactic use)

### Interventional Procedures
- **Urological procedures**: Cystoscopy, urodynamics with pre-medication protocols
- **Anesthetic techniques**: Regional anesthesia for procedures below injury level
- **Botulinum toxin injection**: For detrusor overactivity in recurrent cases

## Common Complications and Their Management

### Acute Complications
- **Hypertensive crisis**: Systolic arterial blood pressure may reach high levels of up to 325 mmHg, requiring immediate medical intervention
- **Cerebral hemorrhage/stroke**: Monitor for neurological changes; emergency imaging if indicated
- **Myocardial infarction**: Cardiac monitoring; troponin levels if chest symptoms
- **Seizures**: Anticonvulsant therapy; address underlying hypertension
- **Retinal hemorrhage**: Ophthalmologic examination for visual complaints

### Chronic Complications
- **Recurrent episodes**: Autonomic dysreflexia can occur in susceptible individuals up to 40 times per day
- **Silent autonomic dysreflexia**: Regular blood pressure monitoring during routine care
- **Cardiovascular morbidity**: Long-term cardiac monitoring and risk factor modification
- **Medication tolerance**: Rotation of antihypertensive agents

## Related Assessment Tools

- **American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale**
- **International Standards to document Autonomic Function following SCI (ISAFSCI)**
- **Autonomic Dysreflexia Questionnaire** (scored 0-24)
- **Composite Autonomic Severity Score (CASS)**
